Project overview

The dashboard answers the question a bank’s management asks every month: did we make the profit we planned, and why not? It is built for a mid-sized commercial bank operating in a high key-rate environment. The page opens on a single headline — net profit for the period and its gap to plan — and then breaks that gap down step by step: from interest income to net profit, by line item, by driver and over time. The interface is in Russian, as it would be for the bank’s own management team.

The dataset is synthetic: 12 months of actual, plan and prior-year P&L, plus average balances for earning assets, loans and equity. I generated it with Python so that every ratio stays within realistic ranges for a Russian bank of this size (NIM 5–6%, cost-to-income 40–48%, cost of risk 1.2–2.2%, ROE 15–22%), with December bonus seasonality and a January slowdown.

Key findings

Ahead of plan for the year, behind in September

Net profit for the 12 months reached ₽34.8B, 3.6% above plan. The bank beat plan in 9 of 12 months, but September closed 5.3% below plan (₽2.86B against ₽3.02B), and Q3 as a whole finished roughly on plan (−0.3%).

Credit quality is the main risk

The September shortfall comes from provisions. Credit loss provisions were ₽411M (31%) above plan. Annualised cost of risk rose from about 1.4% in October–July to 1.9% in August and 2.1% in September. The core business was not the problem: net interest income came in ₽207M above plan in the same month.

Margin pressure from funding costs

Interest expense absorbed 58.8% of interest income, against 56.2% a year earlier. As a result, interest income grew 17% year over year but net profit grew only 10.6%. Net interest margin fell from 5.8% to 5.5% and ROE from 18.8% to 18.1%. Funding costs eased slightly through 2026 and the margin recovered month by month — from 5.1% in October to 6.0% in September.

Costs under control

The cost-to-income ratio stayed close to 42% throughout the year. The only spike was December (46.5%), driven by annual bonuses and year-end administrative spending, which the plan had anticipated.

Conclusion

The bank’s operating engine is healthy: income is growing, costs are disciplined and the interest margin is recovering. The result for the year is ahead of plan. But the late-summer rise in provisions turned September into a miss, and if cost of risk stays above 2%, the full-year advantage over plan will shrink quickly. The dashboard makes that shift visible in one place, with the September shortfall traced back to the provisions line.

Dashboard design & interactivity

  • One executive page. A headline figure with a plain-language explanation underneath, four KPI cards, a profit waterfall, a driver ranking, a monthly trend and the full P&L statement.
  • Period and baseline switches. One set of measures serves the month, quarter-to-date and year-to-date views. Every comparison works against both plan and prior year. Ratios are annualised for each period length.
  • Auto-generated summary. A DAX measure finds the largest negative and largest positive driver and writes the sentence under the headline.
  • Custom visuals in Deneb (Vega-Lite). The built-in Power BI waterfall cannot show intermediate totals, so I built the waterfall, the driver ranking, the monthly trend and the KPI sparklines as Vega-Lite specs.
  • Correct sign convention. Variances are shown by their effect on profit, so higher costs show as negative and lower costs as positive. This matches how finance teams read a P&L.
  • Data model. A fact table with actual, plan and prior-year scenarios, a monthly calendar, and disconnected parameter tables for period, comparison, P&L line structure and waterfall steps.
  • Custom report theme. A warm, low-contrast palette with gold accents. The series colours were checked for colour-vision deficiency and text contrast.
